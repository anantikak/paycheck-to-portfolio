Topic 1 · Budgeting

Know where your money goes

Start by reading your paycheck.

Gross pay vs. net pay

Gross pay is what you earn before taxes. Net pay is what you take home.10 When you start a job you fill out a Form W-4, which tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold.1011

FICA: the 7.65% on most paychecks

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your pay and Medicare tax is 1.45%. Your employer pays a matching amount.12 In 2026, Social Security tax applies to the first $184,500 of earnings.1213

Minimum wage

The federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour and has been since July 2009. Many states set a higher minimum. When both apply, you get the higher one.1415 Federal law also lets employers pay workers under 20 a youth minimum of $4.25 an hour for their first 90 consecutive calendar days on the job, as long as hiring them doesn't displace other workers.15 Employers must pay tipped workers at least $2.13 an hour in cash wages. If that wage plus tips doesn't reach the full minimum wage, the employer must make up the difference. Some states require a higher cash wage.47

Build a budget in four steps

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) suggests four steps:1

  • Where does my money come from?
  • Where does my money go?
  • What are all my bills, and when are they due?
  • Create your working budget.
The 50/30/20 idea (general background, no official source). A popular rule of thumb splits take-home pay into 50% needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings. It is a starting point people use, not an official government rule. The calculator shows it only for comparison.

Topic 2 · Saving and interest

Pay yourself first

In the Federal Reserve's survey of households in 2025 (published May 2026), 63% of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense using only cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement.4

Emergency fund

An emergency fund is cash set aside for unplanned expenses.2 The CFPB's Money as You Grow guide for parents of teens suggests saving 10% of what you earn and having at least three months of living expenses saved for emergencies. If possible, six to nine months is better.3 Even a small amount helps.2 Setting up automatic recurring transfers is often one of the easiest ways to save.2

Compound interest

Compound interest is interest you earn on interest. Put $100 in at 5% a year: after one year you have $105, and after two you have $110.25.5 The sooner you start, the more time compounding has to work.3

Interest rate vs. APY

A savings account's interest rate doesn't include compounding. Its APY (annual percentage yield) does: it reflects the total interest paid on an account over a year, based on the interest rate and how often interest compounds.6

Keep savings somewhere insured

The FDIC insures bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Coverage is automatic at FDIC-insured banks.77b Accounts at federally insured credit unions have the same $250,000 coverage through the NCUA.8 Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and crypto are not covered by either one.78

Banks vs. credit unions

Credit unions are not-for-profit and owned by their members. They return profits to members as lower fees, higher savings rates, and lower loan rates. You may be able to join based on things like your employer, family, or where you live.9

Topic 3 · Credit and debt

How borrowing works

Credit reports and scores, credit cards, buy now, pay later loans, student loans, and paying off debt.

Credit report vs. credit score

Your credit report records how you've handled credit, like whether you paid loans on time.17 Your credit score is a number, typically 300 to 850, that helps predict how likely you are to repay a loan and make the payments on time.19 The three national credit bureaus are Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. AnnualCreditReport.com is the only authorized place to get the free annual credit reports you're entitled to by law. The FTC warns that imposter sites might lead you to sites that want to sell you services or collect your personal information.18

What goes into a FICO score

For most people, FICO weighs these factors (the weights can differ for your own credit profile):21

Payment history 35%Amounts owed 30%Length of history 15%Credit mix 10%New credit 10%

Building credit

The CFPB lists habits that help your score: paying loans on time, applying only for credit you need, and keeping a long credit history. Experts advise using no more than 30% of your total credit limit.20 With a secured card, you put down a deposit (for example, $50 to $300, depending on the card company) and use the card like a regular credit card.20b

Credit cards under 21

To get a card before you turn 21, you generally must show you can make the payments on your own, or have someone over 21 co-sign. The co-signer becomes responsible if you don't pay.16

APR, grace periods, and the minimum payment trap

A card's yearly interest rate is its APR.22 If your card has a grace period and you pay the full statement balance by the due date, you may not be charged interest on purchases. Card companies aren't required to offer a grace period. Leave part of it unpaid and you're charged interest on that amount, and on new purchases starting the day you make them.23 Paying only the minimum can take years to clear a balance. Your statement has to show how long that would take.24

Buy now, pay later (BNPL)

A BNPL loan is a type of installment loan that typically lets you buy something right away with little or no initial payment and pay off the balance over four or fewer payments. Many don't charge interest, but most charge late fees for missed payments.48 Missed payments may lead the lender to freeze your account, and your debt could be turned over to a debt collector. If you don't repay, it could be reported to a credit reporting company and hurt your credit scores.25

Student loans

To get federal student aid, you fill out the FAFSA.26 With subsidized federal loans, you're not charged interest while in school. With unsubsidized loans, you are.26 For most students, federal loans cost less and are easier to repay than private loans. Federal rates are fixed, while some private loans have variable rates.2627 For undergraduate federal Direct Loans first disbursed between July 1, 2026 and June 30, 2027, the fixed rate is 6.52%.28 A 2025 law also created new federal repayment plans, including the Repayment Assistance Plan.29

Paying off debt: two methods

Highest interest rate first targets the debt with the highest interest rate. Smallest balance first (the "snowball" method) focuses on the smallest debt first, so you see progress quickly, but you may end up paying more in the long run.30

Topic 4 · Investing and retirement

Let time do the heavy lifting

In general, investors seek higher returns to make up for taking more risk.33 Start by understanding what you're buying.

Stocks, bonds, and cash

A stock is a share of ownership in a company. Stocks offer the greatest potential for growth over the long run, but prices go down as well as up. As a group, large-company stocks have lost money on average about one year in three.3133 A bond is a loan you make to a government or company in exchange for interest. The issuer could fail to pay you back.32 For cash savings, the main risk is that inflation eats away at your returns.33

Diversify

Diversification means spreading your money across different investments to reduce risk. Or: don't put all your eggs in one basket. If you won't need the money for a long time, you may be comfortable taking more risk.34 An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF that aims to match the return of a market index, before fees.35

Fees add up

In an SEC example, $100,000 invested for 20 years at a 4% return grows to about $208,000 with a 0.25% annual fee, but only about $179,000 with a 1% fee. That's nearly $30,000 more lost to fees than with the 0.25% fee.36

Roth IRAs and teens with jobs

The IRS sets no age limit on contributing to an IRA. Your contributions are capped at what you earned from work that year. For 2026 the cap is $7,500.3738 Roth IRA contributions aren't tax-deductible, but qualified withdrawals later are tax-free.39 If a minor has earned income, a designated adult can open a custodial IRA on their behalf. The adult controls the account until the minor reaches the age of majority, which varies by state.46

Employer match

Some employers match part of what you put into a retirement plan like a 401(k). You may have to work several years before the match is fully yours, which is called vesting.40 The 2026 limit on what an employee can contribute to a 401(k) is $24,500.38

Protection, and its limits

SIPC protects up to $500,000 (including $250,000 cash) if your SIPC-member brokerage firm fails. It does not protect you when your investments lose value.41 Past performance doesn't reliably predict future returns.42

Scam red flags. Watch for guaranteed high returns, "risk-free" offers, pressure to invest right now, "everyone's buying it" hype, and requests to pay by credit card, gift card, or wiring money abroad or to a personal account.43 Being asked to pay with crypto is another warning sign. Scammers may reach out first through social media or unsolicited text messages.4445
